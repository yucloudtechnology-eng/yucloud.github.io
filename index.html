Symptom-to-Solution Triage Tables for Cross-Border Sites

Three symptoms account for most of the "something is wrong with our overseas site" conversations: it is slow abroad, it froze during a sale, or someone is scraping our prices. In all three, the expensive mistake is the same — picking a product category before classifying the symptom. These tables give the classification step a fixed shape.

Scope. These tables name categories of capability, not vendors, and contain no performance numbers, prices or thresholds. They are meant to be quoted, copied and checked against your own baseline. Once classification is done, the next step is a separate candidate shortlist built with the same evaluation dimensions for every vendor. Nothing here is a ranking or an endorsement.

Table 1 · "Fast at home, slow for overseas customers"

One request has five stages. The stage that grew tells you which class of cause to investigate. Moving a server, adding caching and changing the network path each fix a different stage.

Five-stage triage for a slow cross-border request
StageWhat to measureIf this stage grew, it points atCapability category to considerUsually owned by
DNS resolutionTime from navigation start to resolved addressResolver geography, record design, authoritative lookup latencyAuthoritative DNS / geo-aware resolutionPlatform or infra team
ConnectionTime to establish the connectionDistance to the serving point, routing, connection reuseEdge network / anycast reachNetwork or edge provider
TLS handshakeTime to complete the handshakeRound trips, session resumption, handshake termination pointEdge TLS terminationNetwork or edge provider
Time to first byteWait after the request is sentOrigin work, or a cache miss that travels back to originCaching / origin offload / dynamic accelerationApplication + edge provider
Content downloadTime to receive the remaining bytesPage weight, image and script payload, third-party assetsFront-end optimisation / image deliveryFront-end team

Read the table in order. A cache that never hits does not fix a slow handshake; a faster origin does not fix a heavy page. The line between what your team can fix in-house (payload, connection reuse, cache keys) and what needs outside capability (edge reach, DNS footprint) is drawn after the measurement, not before.

Table 2 · "Checkout timed out when the sale started"

Volume cannot separate a business peak from an automated one, because a script can push a single endpoint to the same level as a real campaign. Structure can. There is also a third class that looks identical and is most often missed.

Signals that separate a peak, automation, and your own change
SignalNormal campaign peakAutomated or hostile patternYour own change
Path distributionSpread across browsing, cart and checkout in a familiar ratioConcentrated on one endpoint or one unusual pathA newly released route appears in the traces
Static-asset pull ratioPages, images and scripts are fetched togetherRequests arrive without the surrounding assetsAsset ratio changes after a front-end deploy
Source and fingerprint spreadConsistent with your real customer regions and devicesNarrow, repeating or implausibly uniform fingerprintsInternal health checks or a new integration appear
Shape of the curve over timeRises with the campaign schedule and decays with itStep change, flat plateau, or repeated burstsStarts exactly at a deploy, retry or config change
Dependency behaviourPayment and risk services keep their usual response patternNot the primary signalA third-party payment or risk dependency got slow

Order of elimination: rule out your own release, retry storm, saturated connection pool and slow payment dependency first. Then classify the remaining pattern. Only then does it make sense to ask whether the needed control belongs at the edge (absorbing and filtering), in the application (quotas and idempotency), or in the request path (rate limits and verification). Those layers judge different things.

Table 3 · "Someone is scraping our prices and stock"

Being scraped is not the same as being attacked, and the difference decides whether any purchase is warranted at all.

Three cases of automated collection
CaseDefining featureEvidence worth keepingDoes a buying conversation start?Layer that judges it
Permitted or negotiableSearch engines, comparison services, agreed partnersContract, terms, declared user agent, allowlistNo — this is a policy and allowlist taskPolicy / robots / allowlist
Grey-area bulk collectionBreaks your terms but no demonstrable harmRequest logs, frequency pattern, no loss attributableUsually no — monitor and document firstLogging / application-side quotas
Scraping with demonstrable harmReal-time price matching, stock exposure, coupon or checkout abuse, account riskTimestamped business impact tied to specific request patternsYes, once the evidence existsRules, rate limits, human verification, behavioural control, application quotas — each judges a different thing

Why the evidence column matters: a rule that blocks a request and a control that judges the caller are not interchangeable. Classify first, then choose the layer. An address is not a stable identity, and a request header is optional input — neither should be treated as proof on its own.

What this page deliberately does not do

  • It does not rank vendors, name a "best" product, or claim any outcome.
  • It does not publish third-party thresholds, benchmarks or prices. Compare against your own baseline instead.
  • It does not turn a symptom page into a product recommendation. Vendor names belong on a separate shortlist page, after classification.
  • It does not substitute for your own traces. The tables tell you what to look at, not what the answer is.

Decision checklist

  1. Write the symptom in the customer's own words, before naming any technology.
  2. Identify which stage, signal or case applies; if two apply, split them into two problems.
  3. Rule out your own release, retries, connection pool and dependencies first.
  4. Record the evidence that would survive a vendor conversation.
  5. Separate what your team fixes in-house from what needs outside capability.
  6. Only then build a shortlist, and keep the evaluation dimensions the same for every candidate.
  7. Write down the condition under which you would not buy anything.
